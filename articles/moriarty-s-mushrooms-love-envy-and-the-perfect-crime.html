Moriarty’s Mushrooms: Love, Envy, and the Perfect Crime

Recovered from the original Avinash Holmes website archive.

Disclaimer:
This story is a work of fiction. The events, characters, and substances described are purely imaginary, and any resemblance to real persons or events is coincidental. The inky cap mushroom and its toxic effects when combined with alcohol are factual; however, this story is not intended to encourage or promote the use of these or any other substances to cause harm. The author does not condone or support any actions that could result in injury, harm, or death. Please enjoy this story as a piece of creative writing, and do not attempt to replicate any of the actions described herein.

______________________________________________________

Holmes was a man of paradoxes, a man with many interests. With his tall, lean frame, sharp features, and perpetually furrowed brow, he embodied the look of a classic detective, an impression reinforced by the way he moved through life—quiet, observant, and always thinking several steps ahead. His dark hair, slightly graying at the temples, added to the aura of intellectual intensity that surrounded him. His eyes, a deep brown, were often hidden behind thin, rectangular glasses that he adjusted more out of habit than necessity. Though his real name was Devashish Kapoor, he had adopted the moniker "Holmes" in a nod to his childhood hero, Sherlock Holmes. 

But unlike the famed detective, Devashish found himself drawn not to solving crimes, but to committing them—in his mind, at least. His admiration for Holmes was overshadowed by a growing fascination with Holmes' nemesis, Professor Moriarty. The idea of a mastermind operating in the shadows, capable of orchestrating elaborate schemes with deadly precision, captivated him. This obsession had seeped into every aspect of his life, from his work as a botanist to his writing. He was drafting a novel, a dark, intricate tale of murder, where the killer used the most innocent-looking plants and fungi to eliminate his victims without leaving a trace. 

His obsession with poisons, particularly those derived from fungi and plants, had led him to collect specimens that could kill without leaving a trace. Among these, the inky cap mushroom was the jewel of his collection. Unlike other toxic fungi, its lethal effects were not apparent when consumed raw but became devastatingly effective only after being cooked. This unique characteristic made it the perfect tool for a crime that could easily be mistaken for a natural death—especially when alcohol was involved. His research was meticulous, rooted in his own experiments and discoveries. In truth, the novel was less fiction and more a detailed manual on how to kill without evidence, camouflaged within the trappings of a story.

His workspace, cluttered with books on toxicology, plant biology, and obscure botanical texts, reflected this obsession. Among the pages of his novel, hastily written notes and diagrams of various poisonous plants and fungi could be found, each one annotated with methods of extraction, application, and potential lethality. It was not just a creative outlet; it was his life's work.

Holmes worked in a modest but renowned research lab, where he spent his days studying the deadly secrets of nature. His colleagues, Rohan and Natalia, were the only people who ventured into his otherwise solitary world. Rohan was the antithesis of Holmes—charming, extroverted, and effortlessly popular. Natalia, with her sharp intellect and warm smile, was the light of the lab. Holmes had harbored feelings for her for years, feelings he had never dared to express.

One afternoon, Holmes was hunched over the Nanodrop machine, carefully measuring DNA samples, when he overheard a conversation between Rohan and Natalia. They stood at the adjacent bench, talking in hushed tones, but their words were clear to Holmes.

“So, dinner at my place tonight?” Rohan asked, leaning closer to Natalia with that familiar mischievous grin.

She smiled back, a soft laugh escaping her lips. “I’d love that. What’s on the menu?”

“Mushrooms,” Rohan replied with a wink. “I know you love them.”

Holmes’ heart sank. He pretended to focus on the tiny droplet he was analyzing, but his mind was racing. The pang of jealousy was sharp, cutting through his normally composed demeanor. He had imagined many scenarios where he might ask Natalia out, but he had never had the courage. Now, as he watched Rohan effortlessly charm her, the idea of losing her—though he never really had her—was unbearable. He adjusted his glasses, hiding his clenched jaw and the storm brewing inside him.

Rohan’s words echoed in his mind, a catalyst that set his twisted plan into motion. Mushrooms. How ironic that Rohan had chosen the very thing that Holmes had studied so intimately. And not just any mushrooms—Holmes had the perfect variety in his collection, the inky cap, known for its deadly reaction with alcohol.

Later that day, as they were packing up to leave the lab, Holmes casually mentioned his love for mushrooms, baiting Rohan into a conversation about their shared interest. Rohan, ever the enthusiast, took the bait, and before long, Holmes had invited him over for a “special mushroom tasting” at his home. Rohan eagerly accepted, thrilled by the prospect of trying something new.

When Rohan arrived at Holmes’ home that evening, he was greeted by the warm, earthy scent of mushroom stew simmering on the stove. The small apartment was tidy, its décor simple, almost spartan, with a few old bookshelves lined with leather-bound volumes. Bhoora, Holmes’ scruffy Indian pariah dog, wagged his tail lazily from his corner, where he lay curled up on a worn-out rug. The dog’s presence added a sense of homeliness to the otherwise cold environment.

The dinner was a masterpiece. The stew, rich with the flavors of various mushrooms, was the star of the meal. Holmes watched with hidden satisfaction as Rohan took bite after bite, savoring the dish without the slightest suspicion. Holmes himself ate heartily, knowing that without alcohol, the inky caps were harmless. As the meal came to an end, Holmes suggested continuing their celebration at a local pub.


“Why don’t we head to the pub?” Holmes proposed, his voice casual. “My treat. We can toast to your promotion.”

Rohan, in high spirits, agreed immediately. “Sounds like a plan! I could use a drink after that feast.”

The pub they chose was dimly lit and tucked away in a quiet alley, the kind of place where regulars drank in peace and strangers rarely ventured. Holmes led the way to a small table in the corner, where they ordered drinks. Rohan, in his usual fashion, went for strong spirits, while Holmes stuck to a glass of fruit juice, citing health reasons.

“Come on, Holmes, just one drink won’t kill you,” Rohan teased, raising his glass.

Holmes smiled, shaking his head. “I’m afraid I’ve sworn off the stuff. Trying to live a bit longer, you know?”

 Rohan grinned, his eyes gleaming with mischief. “Oh, come on, Holmes, just one shot won’t kill you. It’s just a little fun.”

Holmes hesitated, his mind racing. He knew that the severity of symptoms from the inky cap mushrooms was directly proportional to the amount of alcohol consumed. With small amounts, the reaction might be mild, but as blood alcohol concentration increased, the effects would become more pronounced. At a concentration of 5 mg/dl, symptoms would start to appear, becoming significant at 50–100 mg/dl. The thought of disulfiram’s connection to myocardial infarction—a heart attack—flashed through his mind. He calculated the risk quickly and decided that one shot wouldn’t hurt him, especially with his plan to escape the scene before the alcohol could take full effect.

“All right, just one,” Holmes agreed, forcing a smile. “But that’s it.”

Rohan cheered and signaled the bartender, who poured them both a shot of strong whiskey. Holmes lifted his glass, clinking it against Rohan’s with a forced grin. As he downed the shot, he mentally braced himself, knowing he would need to act fast. The warmth of the alcohol spread through his chest, and he could feel the first inklings of the reaction beginning. He didn’t have much time.

Within seconds, Holmes clutched his stomach, his face contorting as he pretended to be overwhelmed by nausea. “Oh no... I think I need to...”

He bolted from his seat, rushing toward the restroom. Before he disappeared, he turned to the bartender. “Keep the shots coming for my friend, will you? I’ll take care of the bill.”

Rohan laughed, calling after him, “You lightweight! I’ll show you how it’s done.”

The bartender, amused, began pouring more shots for Rohan, who was more than happy to keep drinking. As Holmes locked himself in the restroom, he leaned against the sink, biting his nails anxiously. His plan was working perfectly, but the tension gnawed at him. Would the experiment succeed? Would Rohan succumb to the fatal reaction, or would it be brushed off as just another unfortunate heart attack?

Holmes splashed water on his face, his mind racing through every possible outcome. He knew that if an autopsy were conducted, it might reveal the interaction between the mushrooms and alcohol. But by making a show of drinking himself and then pretending to be ill, he hoped to create plausible deniability. He wanted to ensure that if Rohan’s death was investigated, no one would suspect foul play, especially from a colleague who had eaten the same meal and consumed alcohol as well.

He made exaggerated retching noises, loud enough to be heard from outside, further solidifying his alibi. Inside, he was a coiled spring of tension, waiting to see if his meticulously crafted plan would yield the desired result.

After a few minutes, Holmes composed himself, wiping his face dry and adjusting his glasses in the mirror. He took a deep breath, steadied his nerves, and returned to the bar where Rohan was still enthusiastically downing shots, blissfully unaware of the lethal consequences he was about to face. Holmes smiled inwardly, knowing that he had just set the final piece of his plan into motion.

Holmes returned to the bar, feigning embarrassment as he rejoined Rohan. “I think I’m done for the night,” he said, with a sheepish smile. “You’re right, I’m a lightweight.”

Rohan laughed, already several shots deep. “No worries, Holmes! More for me!” He raised another glass, his grin wide and confident, oblivious to the ticking clock within him.

Holmes watched as Rohan continued to drink, feeling a strange mix of anticipation and dread. The seconds seemed to stretch into hours, each sip Rohan took bringing him closer to the inevitable. Holmes glanced around the pub, ensuring that no one was paying them undue attention. It was just another night, two colleagues out for drinks, nothing out of the ordinary.

“Ready to call it a night?” Holmes asked after Rohan had finished his tenth shot. He tried to keep his voice steady, casual, though inside, his nerves were taut.

Rohan nodded, swaying slightly as he stood. “Yeah, let’s get out of here.”

As they stepped out into the cool night air, Rohan seemed in high spirits, the alcohol giving him a false sense of energy. They walked in silence for a few minutes, the streets quiet around them. But then, as they turned a corner, Rohan suddenly slowed, his breathing becoming labored.

“Holmes… something’s not right,” Rohan muttered, a hand clutching his chest.

Holmes turned to him, his face carefully controlled, masking the excitement and fear bubbling beneath the surface. “You’ll be fine, Rohan. Just take deep breaths.”

But Rohan’s condition deteriorated rapidly. His steps became unsteady, and before Holmes could react, Rohan collapsed onto the pavement, his face contorted in pain and confusion.

“Holmes... it hurts...” Rohan gasped, struggling to catch his breath.

Holmes knelt beside him, pulling out his phone. “I’m calling an ambulance,” he said, his voice calm, almost detached. He dialed quickly, reporting Rohan’s symptoms with the practiced tone of someone who had seen this scenario play out in his mind a thousand times.

The ambulance arrived swiftly, the paramedics moving efficiently to assess Rohan’s condition. Holmes stepped back, watching as they worked, his expression a mix of concern and helplessness. Rohan was loaded into the ambulance, and Holmes followed, maintaining his role as the worried colleague.

At the hospital, doctors rushed Rohan into the emergency room, but their efforts were in vain. After what felt like an eternity, a doctor emerged, his expression grave. “I’m sorry, Mr. Holmes. Your friend didn’t make it. It appears he suffered a heart attack, likely exacerbated by the alcohol.”

Holmes nodded, his face a mask of sorrow. “I… I can’t believe it. He was so full of life just a few hours ago.”

The doctor placed a reassuring hand on Holmes’ shoulder. “It’s always difficult to lose someone so suddenly. If there’s anything we can do…”

Holmes shook his head, forcing tears to his eyes. “Thank you, doctor. I just… need some time.”

Natalia was the first to call after the news broke. Her voice trembled with shock and grief. “Holmes, I heard about Rohan… I can’t believe it.”

Holmes comforted her as best as he could, his own voice thick with feigned emotion. “It was so sudden. We were just having a drink, and then… he was gone.”

Natalia was devastated, and Holmes played his part perfectly, the grieving colleague who had been there in Rohan’s final moments. In the days that followed, the lab was a somber place, with Natalia and the other staff members in mourning. Holmes offered his condolences, his heart aching in a different way. He had eliminated his rival, but the emptiness remained. Natalia, whom he had long admired, seemed even more distant now, consumed by her grief for Rohan.

Holmes returned to his work, his life resuming its usual rhythm. The antique vial of coprine extract, a macguffin from his story, was carefully placed back in its drawer, a reminder of the power he wielded. It had been a successful experiment, one that proved the theories he had so carefully constructed. But there was no satisfaction in it, only a hollow victory.

At home, Bhoora greeted Holmes as he always did, with wagging tail and trusting eyes. The dog knew nothing of the darkness in his master’s heart, only the kindness that Holmes showed him. As Holmes sat down to continue his novel, he realized that the line between his fiction and reality had blurred. The story he was writing was no longer just a story—it was his life. And now, with Rohan gone, he had all the time in the world to perfect his art of killing without leaving a trace.

But as the weeks passed, Holmes found himself haunted by what he had done. The thrill of planning and executing the perfect crime was replaced by a gnawing sense of emptiness. He had removed Rohan from the picture, but Natalia remained out of reach, her heart still with the man who was now gone. And though Holmes had covered his tracks well, he couldn’t escape the shadow of his own guilt. The more he tried to push it aside, the more it consumed him, until he began to question whether the victory was worth the price he had paid.
