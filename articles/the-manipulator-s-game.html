The Manipulator's Game

Recovered from the original Avinash Holmes website archive.

Dr. Holmes had long been a fixture in the forensic lab, his sharp intellect often overshadowed by the praise of his colleagues. He had grown accustomed to quietly working in the background, his contributions unnoticed amidst the breakthroughs of others. But when Anil Roy joined the lab as a postdoctoral researcher, Holmes found his patience wearing thin.


Anil, with a background in chemistry and a focus on toxicology, had joined the forensic lab for his postdoctoral research on chemical and biological evidence analysis. His expertise quickly became invaluable in cases involving poisons, drugs, and other substances. He had a natural flair for forensic investigation, and his work often provided the crucial evidence needed to solve complex cases. His analyses were meticulous, his conclusions irrefutable, and his results consistently led to praise that earned him recognition from the department, published papers, and invitations to speak at prestigious conferences. Every time he presented his findings, his success seemed to push Holmes further into the shadows, fueling the dark envy that simmered within him.


Holmes, with his usual calculated demeanor, never let his resentment show. He maintained a professional front, offering Anil a smile and a few words of encouragement whenever he achieved something remarkable. But behind that smile was a growing desire to see him falter.


Anil, despite his brilliance, had one peculiar habit. Every other night, he would send Holmes a message—often late in the evening—asking if he had remembered to turn off the gas valve of the Bunsen burner in the lab. “I’m so paranoid,” he would write. “What if I forgot again? Could you check for me?”


At first, Holmes found this quirk of his mildly amusing. But as his successes continued to pile up, those messages became the seeds of a sinister idea. Holmes, after all, had a mind trained in the art of manipulation and deception, a skill honed by his obsession with imagining orchestrating the perfect crime.


One evening, after receiving Anil’s usual message, Holmes walked into the lab. The gas valve was indeed closed, just as he had left it. But instead of reassuring him, Holmes turned the valve on, letting the gas hiss quietly into the empty lab. The next morning, he casually mentioned to Anil that he had found the gas on when he checked.


Anil was horrified. “I could have sworn I turned it off!” he exclaimed, his face pale.


Holmes pretended to be concerned. “It’s easy to forget little things when you’re so busy,” he said kindly. “Maybe you should slow down a bit. You’ve been working too hard.”


But instead of slowing down, Anil became even more obsessive. He double-checked everything, and then checked again. Holmes, ever the supportive colleague, offered to help him keep track of things. And yet, somehow, things continued to go wrong.


One day, Anil carefully stored his critical forensic samples in the minus 20°C freezer, only to find them left out at room temperature the next day. “I must be losing my mind!” he cried, close to tears. “I know I put them in the freezer!”


Holmes was there, as always, to offer a comforting word. “Maybe you’re just tired, Anil. It happens to the best of us.”


But he knew the truth. Each time Anil left the lab, Holmes would quietly sabotage his work. He would switch off equipment, move samples, or tamper with his reagents—just enough to make it seem like Anil was the one making mistakes. He was careful, though, never leaving any evidence that could be traced back to him. His plan was to slowly erode Anil’s confidence, to make him question his own sanity.


As weeks passed, the once-brilliant Anil became a shadow of his former self. The sleepless nights, the constant fear of making mistakes, and the inexplicable failures in his analyses began to take their toll. He started to second-guess every decision, every action. His performance in the lab deteriorated, and the praise he once received began to dwindle. The very traits that had made him so successful—his confidence, his sharp mind—were now slipping away, thanks to the subtle manipulations of Dr. Holmes.


One evening, Anil sent Holmes another frantic message: “Holmes, did I remember to close the gas valve? Please check!”


Holmes, sitting alone in his dimly lit apartment, allowed himself a small smile. He didn’t need to go to the lab to know the answer. He had already ensured that the gas was left on earlier that day. As he composed a reassuring reply, he reflected on the delicate balance of his deception. It was a dangerous game, but one that he played with the precision of a master strategist.


That night, as he walked into the lab, Holmes was greeted by a different scene. The lab was dark, but not empty. Anil stood by the gas valve, his face pale, his hands trembling. He had come to check himself this time. When he saw Holmes, his eyes filled with a mix of relief and confusion.


“Holmes… I was so sure I turned it off this time. I don’t understand what’s happening to me.”


Holmes stepped closer, his expression soft, sympathetic. “Anil, you’re just stressed. Why don’t you take a few days off? Get some rest. The lab can manage without you for a little while.”


Anil nodded, tears welling in his eyes. He was too exhausted, too defeated to argue. As he left the lab that night, he couldn’t shake the feeling that something was deeply wrong—not just with him, but with the world around him.


Holmes watched him go, his heart pounding with the thrill of his victory. He had succeeded in undermining Anil, in making him doubt his own mind. But as the lab door closed behind him, Holmes felt a strange emptiness. The thrill of manipulation was fleeting, and the satisfaction of seeing him fall had left a bitter aftertaste.


The days following Anil’s departure were uneventful for Holmes, or so it seemed. With Anil no longer a constant presence in the lab, Holmes felt an odd sense of triumph mixed with unease. His successes were no longer overshadowed by Anil’s. The lab had become his domain once more, but the thrill of undermining Anil was gone, leaving only a hollow victory. The tension that had once driven him was replaced by a hollow silence. But little did Holmes know, the game he thought he had mastered was far from over.


One morning, as Holmes went through his routine, he noticed something unusual on his desk—a small, neatly wrapped package with a note attached. The handwriting was unmistakably Anil’s. He opened it carefully. It was a book, freshly published, titled The Mind's Labyrinth: A Study of Human Deception. On the note, Anil wrote:


“To Holmes, with my deepest gratitude for being the perfect subject. Chapter 13 is especially enlightening. – Anil Roy”


Holmes stared at the note and the book, his blood running cold, his legs trembling with panic.


Part Two: The Psychologist's Gambit


Anil had always been a brilliant mind, but what Holmes had underestimated was the depth of his intellect beyond the confines of the lab. He was not just a forensic scientist but also a certified psychologist, holding a diploma from Harvard. His fascination with human behavior had led him to study the subtleties of envy, deception, and manipulation. And he had seen the signs in Holmes long before he had begun his subtle attacks.


It had started innocuously enough, with genuine requests for help. Anil’s messages about the gas valve were born from his careful nature, but as he noticed Holmes's growing resentment, he became suspicious. His friendly demeanor masked a deeper, darker jealousy, one that Anil could sense as clearly as he could analyze a toxic substance.


One evening, after another long day in the lab, Anil approached Holmes as he was packing up. "I'm heading out," he said, feigning fatigue. "Could you just check the gas valve for me? I’m not sure if I turned it off."


Holmes, always eager to reinforce Anil’s supposed carelessness, agreed with a nod. Anil left the lab, but instead of going home, he lingered outside the building, his phone in hand. Unbeknownst to Holmes, he had positioned a small, inconspicuous camera facing the gas valve before he left, its lens capturing the entire scene.


As he watched from the shadows, his phone buzzed with a message from Holmes: "The gas was on. I turned it off for you." Anil smiled grimly. The camera had recorded everything—Holmes turning the valve on, then lying to him about it. The evidence was undeniable.


For the next few weeks, Anil played his part perfectly. He acted increasingly distressed, allowing Holmes to believe his tactics were working. He made deliberate mistakes, misplaced items, and faked forgetfulness. He even left fake samples out at room temperature, knowing full well that Holmes would seize the opportunity to tamper with them. All the while, his hidden cameras documented Holmes’s every move.


What Holmes didn’t know was that Anil had confided in his PI, Dr. Raj Roy, who was not only his mentor but also his uncle. Dr. Roy was furious when Anil revealed Holmes’s actions, but Anil convinced him not to confront Holmes directly. He had a different plan in mind, one that would satisfy both his psychological curiosity and his desire to see justice served in his own way.


“Let him play his games,” Anil had told his uncle. “I want to see how far he’ll


 go, how much he’ll reveal about himself. And when he thinks he’s won, we’ll have all the evidence we need.”


Dr. Roy agreed, albeit reluctantly, and together they devised a plan. Anil would fake a breakdown, take time off, and then reappear with a publication that would expose Holmes’s manipulation.


The book The Mind’s Labyrinth was not just a study of human deception; it was a trap, carefully crafted and designed to ensnare Holmes. Anil had spent the past few weeks writing it, meticulously detailing the psychological profile of a manipulator—one that mirrored Holmes’s behavior almost perfectly.


When Holmes received the book, he was too rattled to respond. The title alone sent a chill down his spine, but it was Chapter 13 that truly unsettled him. It was a detailed account of Anil’s experience with a manipulative colleague, told in such a way that there was no doubt about the identity of the antagonist.


In the following weeks, the tension in the lab grew unbearable. Anil had returned, his demeanor calm, almost unnervingly so. He spoke little of his time off, but his presence was enough to unnerve Holmes. The lab techs began to notice Holmes’s increasing paranoia, his short temper, and his reluctance to engage with Anil.


The climax came during a departmental meeting. Anil presented a paper, co-authored with Dr. Roy, on the psychological effects of workplace manipulation on young scientists. The room was silent as Anil detailed the methods used by manipulative colleagues to undermine their peers. The descriptions were eerily specific, and as Anil spoke, Holmes could feel the eyes of his colleagues turning towards him.


By the end of the presentation, Holmes knew he had been outplayed. His PI, Dr. Raj Roy, congratulated Anil for his book and announced that Anil had received an invitation from Harvard to present at a psychology conference. The room erupted in applause, and Holmes’s face turned crimson with shame.


As Anil concluded his presentation, he received a final, haunting message from Holmes’s phone: “You should have known, Holmes. The mind is the most dangerous weapon of all.”


And with that, Holmes walked out of the forensic lab for the last time, the weight of his actions pressing down on him like a leaden shroud. The game was over, and he had lost. 



The end

About the Author

Dr. Avinash Sharma

Avinash Sharma earned his Ph.D. from Ben-Gurion University of the Negev, Israel. He is currently a postdoctoral researcher in the aging research laboratory at Brown University, Rhode Island, USA. Dr. Sharma is passionate about traveling, photography, and writing science articles focused on health and the science of aging. In addition, he has a keen interest in crafting science fiction and crime thriller stories, combining his scientific knowledge with a flair for suspense and creativity.
